Perception Lab

The science of hearing what isn't there — and why it's the blueprint for a listening metric. Layered: each depth goes further than the last. Play the demos at Depth 1 before reading anything else. Progress tracking for these resources lives in the MATH_BRUSHUP.html perception track.

Depth 0 · The claim

One paragraph. Everything below is evidence for it.

Perception is not reception. Your auditory system does not deliver sound to you — it delivers its best guess about what caused the sound, computed from a prior built over a lifetime. The percept you experience is the guess, not the signal. When signal is ambiguous, degraded, or missing, the prior fills in — confidently, seamlessly, without a warning flag.

Which is exactly what a neural codec does (ENCODER_ANATOMY.html §6, the Monet principle): describe every input in the vocabulary of its training world, nearest-expressible-sound, no error signal. Your brain is an encoder trained on your native language. The demos below let you catch it in the act.

Depth 1 · Feel it — the playable illusions

Each demo isolates one piece of the machinery. ~1 hour total to play all six.

Sine-wave speech — installing a prior in one shot

Speech reduced to three whistling sine tracks. First listen: alien R2-D2 chirps. Then you hear the original sentence once. Now replay the whistles — they are speech, permanently. The signal never changed; your decoder did. This is the cleanest demonstration that the percept lives in the prior, not the waveform.

Thesis link: the ON/OFF-manifold boundary is movable. One exposure dragged the whistles onto your speech manifold. A codec's manifold is frozen at train time — it can never have this "aha" — but a metric with test-time adaptation (anchor D, the iterative listener) could.
Go deeper: Chris Darwin's SWS demo page (Sussex) · if the link is down, go via users.sussex.ac.uk/~cjd

Phonemic restoration — hearing a phoneme that was deleted

Cut the /s/ out of "legi_lature", splice a cough over the gap. Listeners hear the /s/ — clearly — and cannot locate the cough. The brain reconstructs the missing phoneme from context and presents the reconstruction as sensation. Warren, 1970; still startling.

Thesis link: this is why "the codec is contractually obligated to encode the damage" matters. Your ears would repair a dropout; the codec must transmit it. The metric reads the codec precisely because it doesn't hallucinate the fix — and human MOS raters partially do, which Gate 2 will have to live with.
Go deeper: DePaul interactive demo — ~10 min

Yanny/Laurel & Green Needle/Brainstorm — ambiguity resolved by prior

One clip, two stable percepts. Yanny/Laurel splits on which frequency band your system weights (age, playback device, expectation). Green Needle/Brainstorm is stronger: the word you think about is the word you hear — attention selects the prior, the prior writes the percept, on demand, reversibly.

Thesis link: a single latent point decoded two ways by two priors — the routing picture (anchor B). Ambiguity isn't noise on one answer; it's two experts claiming the same input. Routing entropy is the honest output there, not a forced label.
Go deeper: Indiana U dual-ambiguity player (both illusions, frequency sliders) · NYT Yanny/Laurel slider (may paywall — the IU player covers it)

McGurk effect — vision overwrites audition

Audio says "ba", lips say "ga", you hear "da" — a sound present in neither stream. Knowing the trick doesn't break it. The percept is a fusion computed below the level you can inspect: priors from one modality re-writing "raw" sensation in another.

Thesis link: percepts are committee outputs, not sensor readings. Any metric that claims to predict "what a human hears" is predicting an inference, not an acoustic fact — the deep reason reference-free quality metrics are possible at all.
Go deeper: BBC Horizon demo — 5 min

Speech-to-song — repetition flips the category

Diana Deutsch's "sometimes behave so strangely": a spoken phrase, looped. Around the 4th repetition it becomes melody — and once flipped, the original full sentence sounds like the speaker bursts into song mid-sentence. Zero signal change; pure recategorization.

Thesis link: evidence accumulation with hysteresis (anchor D). The percept is a running posterior that commits — more input can flip it, and the flip sticks. One-pass feedforward codecs have no analog of "listening again".
Go deeper: Deutsch's demo page (UCSD) · browser may warn about the site certificate — the audio is fine

Categorical perception — the /ba/–/pa/ cliff

Voice-onset time is a continuous physical knob. Sweep it and your percept doesn't sweep — it jumps: /ba/, /ba/, /ba/, /pa/. Discrimination is razor-sharp at the boundary and nearly blind inside a category. The continuous world arrives; a discrete world is delivered.

Thesis link: this is quantization — RVQ codebooks do the same thing to latent space (snap continuous z to discrete codes). Depth taps d1…d8 ask how much of the continuum survives the snapping; Gate 1 severity asks the same about the damage dose.
Go deeper: Emory interactive VOT experiment (run it on yourself, ~15 min) · UMN open-textbook chapter

Depth 2 · How the prior gets built — the developmental evidence

You weren't born with the cliff. You carved it — and paid for it with the axes you pruned.

Kuhl's perceptual magnet & Werker's narrowing

The universal infant. At 6 months, babies discriminate phoneme contrasts from any language — Hindi dental/retroflex, Salish uvulars, all of it. They are, briefly, citizens of every phonology.

The narrowing (Werker). Between 6 and 12 months, discrimination of non-native contrasts collapses while native contrasts sharpen. The general-purpose ear is traded for a specialized one — a feature, not a loss: specialization is what makes native speech perception fast and noise-robust.

The magnet (Kuhl). The mechanism: native-language prototypes act like magnets, warping perceptual space so nearby sounds get pulled toward the prototype and become indistinguishable from it. An adult Japanese listener doesn't ignore the /r/–/l/ difference — the axis it lived on has been collapsed. The information arrives at the cochlea and dies in the encoder.

Thesis link: this is the Monet principle with a growth curve. Training prunes the basis to the training manifold — infant → adult is exactly random-init → converged codec. "Hearing Swahili through English ears" is the documented adult condition, and WavLM discarding noise while keeping phonetics is the same trade Werker's infants made.
Go deeper: Kuhl, PNAS — a new view of language acquisition · Werker, PNAS — perceptual narrowing (both open, skim figures first)

Depth 3 · The theory that unifies it — perception as controlled hallucination

One framework predicts every demo above.

Predictive coding

The brain doesn't process sound bottom-up. It runs a generative model downward — "here is what I expect to hear next" — and the upward signal carries only the prediction error. Perception is the model's running best guess, continuously corrected by error. Anil Seth's phrase: a controlled hallucination — controlled by the world, but authored by the model.

prior (expectation)  ──predict──▶  expected sound
                                        │ compare
actual sound  ─────────────────▶  prediction error  ──update──▶  percept

Every Depth-1 demo is this loop caught mispredicting: restoration = error silenced by strong prior; sine-wave speech = new prior installed, error vanishes; Green Needle = attention selects which prior predicts; McGurk = visual prediction wins the arbitration; speech-to-song = the posterior flips basins.

Thesis link: anchor D verbatim. Analysis-by-synthesis — encode → predict → compare → re-encode the residual — is a metric architecture: spend compute where prediction error stays high, exactly like a human leaning closer to a bad phone line.
Go deeper: Anil Seth, TED — your brain hallucinates your conscious reality (17 min, the canonical intro) · TED-Ed animated version (5 min)

Depth 4 · The machinery — three tricks the codec doesn't have

The ML vocabulary for each brain trick. This is where the thesis anchors live.

Routing — mixture of experts (→ anchor B)

The polyglot doesn't average Russian and Ukrainian priors — a gate picks one, provisionally, and can switch ("wait, that word is Ukrainian"). In ML: mixture-of-experts — a small gating network routes each input to specialized sub-models; only the chosen experts fire. The gate's entropy is a confidence signal for free: sharp routing = sure; split routing = genuinely ambiguous input.

Go deeper: Grootendorst — A Visual Guide to Mixture of Experts (50+ diagrams, 30 min — the best visual MoE intro that exists)

Evidence accumulation — drift-diffusion (→ anchor D)

"Let me hear more" formalized: evidence accumulates as a noisy random walk toward one of two decision boundaries; hit a boundary, commit. Drift rate = evidence quality; boundary height = caution. Fits human reaction times and error patterns eerily well — time-to-decision is itself a confidence readout.

Go deeper: Frontiers tutorial — drift-diffusion without heavy math (20 min) · interactive visualizer paper (drag drift/boundary/noise, watch RT distributions move)

Metacognition — knowing what you don't know (→ anchor C)

Hearing a language you can't even place, you know two things at once: (1) it's speech, (2) you don't speak it. That second signal — confidence about your own perception — is metacognition, and it's the channel codecs entirely lack. A codec always outputs its best Monet; it has no "I am off my manifold" wire. Humans have it, imperfectly — we're systematically overconfident, which is its own lesson for calibrating any confidence output.

Thesis link: anchor C is literally "build this wire": an explicit distance-from-manifold output validated on known OOD before it judges generated audio. The missing organ, made first-class.
Go deeper: What Is Metacognition (10 min intro) · Veritasium — why people are so confident when they're wrong (Dunning-Kruger, 20 min)
